\section{The Canonical Ensemble}

\paragraph{Constraining the average of an observable.}
The microcanonical ensemble does not distinguish between accessible
microstates, so only their number matters. However, realistic systems obey
constraints imposed by measurement. Suppose microstate $i$ has a definite
value $a_i$ of an observable $A$, and the measured average is $\bar A$. The
expectation value is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\langle A\rangle \equiv \sum_i p_i a_i,}
\end{equation}
and the constraint reads $\langle A\rangle=\bar A$.
\textcolor{red}{Here, $\langle A\rangle$ denotes the operation that extracts
the mean of the observable $A$, and $\bar A$ denotes the numerical value
assigned to that mean. Since only one constraint is present, the multiplier
$\lambda_a$ is renamed $\beta$.}
The Lagrangian now contains the multiplier $\alpha$ for normalization and
the multiplier $\beta$ for the constraint on $\langle A\rangle$. The
stationarity conditions of this Lagrangian are
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial p_i}
    &= -\ln p_i-1-\alpha-\beta a_i=0, \\
    \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha}
    &= -\Big(\sum_i p_i-1\Big)=0, \\
    \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta}
    &= -\Big(\sum_i a_i p_i-\bar A\Big)=0.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{The Boltzmann distribution and the partition function.}
The first condition gives $p_i = e^{-1-\alpha-\beta a_i}$. Substitution of
this result into the normalization condition and the constraint yields
\begin{align*}
    e^{-1-\alpha}\sum_i e^{-\beta a_i} = 1,
    \qquad
    e^{-1-\alpha}\sum_i a_i e^{-\beta a_i} = \bar A.
\end{align*}
The first equation determines $\alpha$ in terms of $\beta$ and leaves a
single free parameter. The \textcolor{blue}{canonical partition function} is
defined as
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{Z(\beta) \equiv \sum_i e^{-\beta a_i},}
\end{equation}
so the probability distribution becomes
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta a_i}}{Z(\beta)}.}
\end{equation}
This result is the \textcolor{blue}{Boltzmann distribution}. For $\beta>0$,
states with larger $a_i$ are less probable. The remaining constraint,
$\langle A\rangle(\beta)=\bar A$, determines $\beta$.

\paragraph{Uniqueness of the multiplier.}
The uniqueness of $\beta$ follows from the mean expressed through $Z$:
\begin{align*}
    \langle A\rangle = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_i a_i e^{-\beta a_i}
    = -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}.
\end{align*}
Hence
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\langle A\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}.}
\end{equation}
With $\mathrm{Var}(A)\equiv\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2$, the
derivative of the mean is
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial\langle A\rangle}{\partial\beta}
    &= \frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}
    \left(\frac{\sum_i a_i e^{-\beta a_i}}{Z}\right) \\
    &= -\frac{\sum_i a_i^2 e^{-\beta a_i}}{Z}
    +\frac{1}{Z^2}\left(\sum_i a_i e^{-\beta a_i}\right)^2 \\
    &= -\langle A^2\rangle+\langle A\rangle^2
    = -\mathrm{Var}(A),
\end{align*}
so
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\frac{\partial\langle A\rangle}{\partial\beta} = -\mathrm{Var}(A).}
\end{equation}
Since $\mathrm{Var}(A)\geq0$, $\langle A\rangle(\beta)$ is monotonically
non-increasing. The mean is strictly decreasing whenever $A$ takes more than
one value over the accessible microstates. Thus, for target values in the
interior of the achievable range, $\langle A\rangle$ determines $\beta$
uniquely.

\paragraph{Entropy of the canonical distribution and the meaning of $\beta$.}
The entropy of the resulting distribution, with $k_B$ omitted during the
derivation, is
\begin{align*}
    S &= -\sum_i p_i\ln p_i
    = -\sum_i p_i\left(-\beta a_i-\ln Z\right) \\
    &= \beta\sum_i a_i p_i+\ln Z\sum_i p_i
    = \beta\langle A\rangle+\ln Z.
\end{align*}
With $k_B$ restored,
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{S=k_B\left(\beta\langle A\rangle+\ln Z\right).}
\end{equation}
Although $S$ depends on $\bar A$ both explicitly and implicitly through
$\beta(\bar A)$, the derivative of $S$ reduces to $\beta$:
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial S}{\partial\langle A\rangle}
    &= \beta
    +\langle A\rangle\frac{d\beta}{d\langle A\rangle}
    +\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}\frac{d\beta}{d\langle A\rangle} \\
    &= \beta
    +\langle A\rangle\frac{d\beta}{d\langle A\rangle}
    -\langle A\rangle\frac{d\beta}{d\langle A\rangle}
    = \beta.
\end{align*}
With $k_B$ restored, this result gives the meaning of the multiplier:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\beta=\frac{1}{k_B}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\langle A\rangle}.}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Concavity of the entropy.}
A function $f$ is \textcolor{blue}{concave} on an interval $I$ if, for all
$x,y\in I$ and all $t\in[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{f\big(tx+(1-t)y\big)\;\geq\;t\,f(x)+(1-t)\,f(y).}
\end{equation}
Geometrically, every chord lies on or below the graph. The function is
\textcolor{blue}{strictly concave} if the inequality is strict for $x\neq y$
and $t\in(0,1)$. For a twice-differentiable $f$, concavity is equivalent to
$f''\leq0$, and $f''<0$ guarantees strict concavity. For $S$ as a function
of $\langle A\rangle$, the inverse-function rule and
$\partial\langle A\rangle/\partial\beta=-\mathrm{Var}(A)$ give
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial\langle A\rangle^2}
    = \frac{d\beta}{d\langle A\rangle}
    = \left(\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{d\beta}\right)^{-1}
    = -\frac{1}{\mathrm{Var}(A)}.
\end{align*}
With $k_B$ restored,
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial\langle A\rangle^2}
    = -\frac{k_B}{\mathrm{Var}(A)}<0.}
\end{equation}
The entropy is therefore strictly concave in $\langle A\rangle$ whenever $A$
takes more than one value over the accessible microstates. This concavity is
the monotonic decrease of $\langle A\rangle(\beta)$ viewed from the side of
$S$, with each value of $\langle A\rangle$ corresponding to exactly one
$\beta$.

\paragraph{Specialization to the internal energy.}
Since the observable $A$ was never specified, the derivation applies to any
observable with a constrained expectation value. The canonical ensemble
conventionally fixes the volume $V$ and the particle number $N$ and
constrains the internal energy. \textcolor{red}{From here on, $A=U$, and
$U_i$ replaces $a_i$.} The results become
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
        Z(\beta) &= \sum_i e^{-\beta U_i}, &
        p_i &= \frac{e^{-\beta U_i}}{Z(\beta)}, \\
        \langle U\rangle &= -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}, &
        S &= k_B\left(\beta\langle U\rangle+\ln Z\right).
    \end{aligned}}
\end{equation}
Section~\ref{sec:multipliers} identifies $\beta$ with the temperature. The
end of this section explains why the particle number or the volume cannot be
constrained alone.

\paragraph{Recovering the microcanonical ensemble.}
If every accessible microstate has the same energy $U$, the canonical
ensemble reduces to the microcanonical ensemble. All the weights
$e^{-\beta U}$ are identical, so the distribution is uniform and the mean
energy equals $U$ exactly. Then
\begin{align*}
    Z &= \sum_{i=1}^{\Omega} e^{-\beta U} = \Omega\, e^{-\beta U}, \\
    S &= k_B\left(\beta U+\ln\Omega-\beta U\right) = k_B\ln\Omega.
\end{align*}

\subsection{First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics --- Meaning of the Multipliers}
\label{sec:multipliers}

\paragraph{The multipliers need thermodynamics for their meaning.}
The MaxEnt formalism does not by itself provide the physical interpretation
or the numerical calibration of the multipliers. In particular, the
identification
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\beta=\frac{1}{k_B}\frac{\partial S}{\partial \langle U\rangle}=\frac{1}{k_BT}}
\end{equation}
cannot be derived from MaxEnt alone. This identification is an empirical
correspondence established by classical thermodynamics. The physical meaning
of the multipliers therefore comes from the thermodynamic laws.

\paragraph{From the first and second laws to the fundamental relation.}
The \textcolor{blue}{first law} expresses energy conservation:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{dU = \delta Q-\delta W+\mu\, dN,}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\delta W=P\,dV$ is the work done \emph{by} the system.
    \item The symbols $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ emphasize that heat and work
    are not state functions, because their values depend on the path
    between two states.
    \item The \textcolor{blue}{chemical potential} $\mu$ is the change in
    internal energy per added particle at fixed entropy and volume.
\end{itemize}
The \textcolor{blue}{second law}, in the form of the Clausius inequality,
states
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\delta Q\leq T\,dS,}
\end{equation}
with equality for reversible processes. For a reversible process, the two
laws combine to give
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{dU=T\,dS-P\,dV+\mu\, dN,}
    \label{eq:combined-law}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\bigg|_{V,N}=T, \qquad
    \frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\bigg|_{S,N}=-P, \qquad
    \frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\bigg|_{S,V}=\mu.}
    \label{eq:U-derivs}
\end{equation}
The equivalent entropy form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:combined-law} is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{dS=\frac{1}{T}\,dU+\frac{P}{T}\,dV-\frac{\mu}{T}\,dN,}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\bigg|_{V,N}=\frac{1}{T}, \qquad
    \frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\bigg|_{U,N}=\frac{P}{T}, \qquad
    \frac{\partial S}{\partial N}\bigg|_{U,V}=-\frac{\mu}{T}.}
    \label{eq:S-derivs}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Extensive and Intensive Variables}

\paragraph{Extensive and intensive variables and their conjugate pairs.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A quantity $X$ is \textcolor{blue}{extensive} if, for two weakly
    coupled subsystems, $X_{\mathrm{total}}=X_1+X_2$. Equivalently, for
    $\lambda$ identical, non-interacting copies, $X_\lambda=\lambda X_1$.
    \item A quantity $Y$ is \textcolor{blue}{intensive} if $Y$ does not scale
    with the number of copies, $Y_\lambda=Y_1$.
    \item Energy, particle number, volume and entropy are extensive, whereas
    temperature, pressure and chemical potential are intensive.
\end{itemize}
This distinction explains why the Lagrange multipliers associated with
extensive constraints are intensive. For any observable $A$ with constrained
average $\langle A\rangle$, the multiplier is
\begin{equation*}
    \beta_A\equiv\frac{1}{k_B}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\langle A\rangle}.
\end{equation*}
Because $\langle A\rangle$ and $S$ are both extensive, their scaling factors
cancel in the derivative, so $\beta_A$ is intensive. Thermodynamic variables
therefore come in \textcolor{blue}{conjugate pairs}, each consisting of an
extensive variable and its \textcolor{blue}{intensive conjugate}.

\paragraph{Additivity of entropy follows from statistical independence.}
\textcolor{red}{Here, the superscripts $(1)$ and $(2)$ also label the
distributions of the two systems, $p^{(1)}_i$ and $p^{(2)}_j$.}
The partition function factorizes because the joint distribution
factorizes, $p_{ij}=p^{(1)}_i\,p^{(2)}_j$. The entropy is then additive:
\begin{align*}
    S_{1+2}
    = -\sum_{i,j} p^{(1)}_i p^{(2)}_j
    \ln\!\big(p^{(1)}_i p^{(2)}_j\big)
    = S_1+S_2.
\end{align*}
Additivity requires statistical independence but not equilibrium, so the two
systems may have different multipliers $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{S_{1+2}=k_B\left(\beta_1\langle U_1\rangle+\ln Z_1
    +\beta_2\langle U_2\rangle+\ln Z_2\right).}
\end{equation}
Only when $\beta_1=\beta_2=\beta$, that is, when the two systems are in
thermal equilibrium, does the joint system become canonical with a single
multiplier:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{S_{1+2}=k_B\left[\beta\langle U_{1+2}\rangle+\ln Z_{1+2}\right],
    \qquad U_{1+2}=U_1+U_2.}
\end{equation}
Independence holds when the coupling between the systems is weak. For strong
coupling, the joint distribution does not factorize, and
$S_{1+2}=S_1+S_2-I$ with the mutual information $I\geq0$.

\subsection{Energy Fluctuation, Thermodynamic Limit and Equivalence of Ensembles}

\paragraph{Energy fluctuation and the heat capacity.}
The variance of the internal energy, the \textcolor{blue}{energy
fluctuation} around the controlled mean $\langle U\rangle=\bar U$, can be
expressed through familiar thermodynamic quantities. By the chain rule,
\begin{align*}
    \mathrm{Var}(U)
    = -\frac{\partial\langle U\rangle}{\partial\beta}
    = -\frac{\partial\langle U\rangle}{\partial T}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\beta}.
\end{align*}
Since $T=1/(k_B\beta)$, $\partial T/\partial\beta=-k_BT^2$. The
\textcolor{blue}{isochoric heat capacity} is defined as
$C_V\equiv\left.\partial\langle U\rangle/\partial T\right|_{V,N}$, at the
fixed volume and particle number of the canonical ensemble. Substitution
gives
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\mathrm{Var}(U) = k_B T^2\, C_V.}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Why fluctuations vanish in the thermodynamic limit.}
The smallness of fluctuations is sometimes attributed to the tiny numerical
value of $k_B$ in SI units. This attribution is incorrect, because the value
of $k_B$ only reflects the choice of units. The genuine reason is that $C_V$
and $\langle U\rangle$ are both extensive and scale linearly with the
particle number $N$. Hence
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(U)}}{\langle U\rangle}
    \sim\frac{\sqrt{N}}{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}},}
\end{equation}
which vanishes as $N\to\infty$. The relative energy fluctuations of a
macroscopic system are therefore negligible because of the sheer size of
$N$. \textcolor{red}{For this reason, $U$ replaces $\langle U\rangle$ from
here on, and likewise for other extensive variables.}
At large $N$, the canonical ensemble therefore behaves as the microcanonical
ensemble, with negligible fluctuations and a one-to-one relation between
energy and temperature.

\paragraph{Physical reading of the fluctuation formula.}
The multiplier $\beta$ is smaller at higher temperature, so the entropic
cost per unit of exchanged energy is smaller. The system then absorbs or
releases energy more freely, consistent with the larger fluctuation
$k_BT^2C_V$.

\paragraph{Concavity of $S(U)$ and thermodynamic stability.}
For $A=U$, the concavity result and $\mathrm{Var}(U)=k_BT^2C_V$ give
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial U^2}
    = -\frac{k_B}{\mathrm{Var}(U)} = -\frac{1}{C_V\,T^2}.}
\end{equation}
Concavity of $S(U)$ is therefore equivalent to $C_V>0$, the
\textcolor{blue}{thermodynamic stability} condition, which requires that
adding energy to a system raises the system temperature. This property is
used again for the Legendre transformation in the next section.

\subsection{Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics}

\paragraph{Two bodies exchanging energy.}
Two bodies, $(1)$ and $(2)$, are in contact and can exchange energy. Two
points need precise statements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The combined system $(1)+(2)$ is genuinely isolated and exchanges
    nothing with the outside. The total energy is a hard constraint,
    $U_1+U_2=U_{\text{tot}}$, so whatever one body releases, the other
    receives. This constraint is a microcanonical condition on the pair as a
    whole.
    \item Each body has an entropy function $S_i(U_i,V_i,N_i)$, which is the
    canonical entropy derived earlier in this section. Each body carries the
    multiplier $\beta_i\equiv k_B^{-1}\,\partial S_i/\partial U_i$ attached to
    the energy of that body. The pair is microcanonical, whereas each body
    behaves canonically, with the other body as the energy reservoir.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Equilibrium requires equal multipliers.}
The two bodies are weakly coupled, so the combined entropy is additive,
$S=S_1+S_2$. Equilibrium corresponds to the maximum of $S$ with respect to
how $U_{\text{tot}}$ is shared. The substitution $U_2=U_{\text{tot}}-U_1$
and differentiation with respect to $U_1$ give
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial S}{\partial U_1}
    = \frac{\partial S_1}{\partial U_1}
    +\frac{\partial S_2}{\partial U_2}\frac{\partial U_2}{\partial U_1}
    = \frac{\partial S_1}{\partial U_1}-\frac{\partial S_2}{\partial U_2}=0.
\end{align*}
Since $\beta_i=k_B^{-1}\,\partial S_i/\partial U_i$, this condition gives
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\beta_1=\beta_2 \qquad\text{and hence}\qquad T_1=T_2.}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Direction of energy flow and the zeroth law.}
Suppose $\beta_1\neq\beta_2$ initially. A small energy transfer $\delta U_1$
into body $(1)$ changes the total entropy by
\begin{equation*}
    \delta S = k_B(\beta_1-\beta_2)\,\delta U_1.
\end{equation*}
The second law requires $\delta S\geq0$, so energy flows into the body with
the larger $\beta$, that is, the lower $T$. Energy therefore flows from high
temperature to low temperature. A third body in contact with both bodies
reaches the same $\beta$ at equilibrium by the same argument. This
transitivity is the \textcolor{blue}{zeroth law of thermodynamics}.

\paragraph{Volume and particle exchange cannot be separated from energy exchange.}
Suppose the two bodies can also exchange volume through a movable partition,
with $V_1+V_2=V_{\text{tot}}$ fixed. Volume exchange does not by itself
imply thermal contact. A heat-insulating piston that slides freely permits
volume exchange but forbids heat exchange, so $T_1\neq T_2$ in general. The
multiplier conjugate to volume is
$\beta_V\equiv k_B^{-1}\,\partial S/\partial V=P/(k_BT)$. The maximum of
$S=S_1(V_1)+S_2(V_2)$ over the split of $V_{\text{tot}}$, with $U_1$ and
$U_2$ fixed, satisfies
\begin{align*}
    \beta_{V,1}=\beta_{V,2}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \frac{P_1}{T_1}=\frac{P_2}{T_2}.
\end{align*}
This condition permits $P_1\neq P_2$. However, by Newton's second law alone,
a partition under a genuine pressure imbalance cannot rest. The flaw is the
assumption that $U_1$ and $U_2$ stay fixed while the partition moves. A
sliding wall does work $P\,dV$ on the side toward which the wall moves, so even a
heat-insulating wall exchanges energy through work, $dU=-P\,dV$ for
$\delta Q=0$. If energy and volume can both be exchanged, as for a movable
wall that also conducts heat, the maximum of $S_1(U_1,V_1)+S_2(U_2,V_2)$
over both splits satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{T_1=T_2 \quad\text{and}\quad P_1=P_2,}
\end{equation}
the physically sensible free-piston equilibrium.

\paragraph{Why the multipliers of $V$ and $N$ contain the temperature.}
More generally, volume or particle exchange always involves energy exchange.
Each exchange contributes its own term to the first law, $-P\,dV$ or
$\mu\,dN$, so the energy varies whenever either quantity varies freely. The
multipliers conjugate to volume and particle number therefore always appear
together with the temperature.
