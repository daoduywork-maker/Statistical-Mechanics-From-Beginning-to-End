\section{Entropy Maximization Principle}

\paragraph{The conventional approach and its limitation.}
The conventional introduction to statistical mechanics rests on four notions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textcolor{blue}{microstate} is a complete description of every
    microscopic parameter of the system.
    \item In an isolated system, all microstates are equally likely.
    \item A \textcolor{blue}{macrostate} is a group of microstates that share
    the same macroscopic observables.
    \item Macrostates that contain more microstates are observed more often,
    so the observed macrostate is the most probable one.
\end{itemize}
Boltzmann, Gibbs and other founders of thermodynamics developed this
approach. The approach is physically intuitive. However, an explanation of
why a system settles into the most probable macrostate requires
\textcolor{blue}{ergodicity} and dynamical arguments, which are difficult to
make rigorous.

\paragraph{The MaxEnt alternative.}
This document follows Jaynes in treating equilibrium statistical mechanics
as a constrained optimization problem, in which the entropy is maximized
subject to what is known macroscopically about the system. This approach is
called the \textcolor{blue}{Entropy Maximization Principle (MaxEnt)}.

\subsection{Gibbs--Shannon Entropy}

\paragraph{From ``disorder'' to ignorance.}
The meaning of \textcolor{blue}{entropy} has long been debated. The popular
description of entropy as ``disorder'' is not a definition, because the description gives
no quantitative measure of disorder. Clausius, Boltzmann and other founders
regarded entropy as a driving force of thermodynamic processes, which left
open the question of which physical quantity entropy encodes. Jaynes instead
argued that entropy is not a property of the body itself but a measure of
the observer's \textcolor{blue}{ignorance} about the body. The
\textcolor{blue}{Gibbs--Shannon entropy} formalizes this idea for $\Omega$
events that occur with probabilities $p(i)$:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{S(p) = -\sum_{i=1}^{\Omega} p(i)\ln p(i).}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Why the Gibbs--Shannon entropy measures ignorance.}
The term $-\ln p(i)$, called the \textcolor{blue}{surprisal} of event $i$,
measures the ignorance about that event:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A certain event has $p(i)=1$, so the surprisal is $-\ln 1 = 0$.
    \item A rare event has a small $p(i)$, so the surprisal is large.
\end{itemize}
A direct sum of $-\ln p(i)$ would fail, because knowing that an event will
occur and knowing that the event will not occur both mean low ignorance. Each term
is therefore weighted by $p(i)$. Rare events seldom occur, and near-certain
events are already expected, so both contribute little to the total. Thus,
$S$ is the expected surprisal, $\langle -\ln p\rangle$.

\subsection{Entropy Maximization}

\paragraph{The principle of maximum ignorance.}
Jaynes' second idea is to choose the distribution that maximizes the
ignorance, that is, the Gibbs--Shannon entropy $S(p)$, subject to the known
constraints whenever the underlying mechanism is unknown. A lower-entropy
distribution would introduce an unexplained bias toward some events. For
example, the two outcomes of a coin flip should have equal probabilities
unless a constraint, such as a loaded coin, states otherwise.

\paragraph{Formulating the constrained optimization.}
\textcolor{red}{For simplicity, $p_i$ replaces $p(i)$ from now on, because
only one probability distribution appears.}
Each known constraint is written as $g_a(p)=0$. For a constraint on the
expectation value of an observable that takes the value $g_a(i)$ in event
$i$,
\begin{equation*}
    g_a(p) = \sum_i p_i\, g_a(i) - \langle g_a\rangle .
\end{equation*}
Lagrange multipliers remove the constraints through the Lagrangian
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\mathcal{L}(p)
    = -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i
    - \alpha\left(\sum_i p_i - 1\right)
    - \sum_a \lambda_a\, g_a(p),}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ enforces normalization and each $\lambda_a$ enforces one
constraint. The maximizing distribution satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p_i} = 0,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \alpha} = 0,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda_a} = 0.}
    \label{eq:conditions}
\end{equation}
The last two conditions reproduce normalization and the constraints. The
first condition determines $p_i$.

\paragraph{The general MaxEnt distribution.}
For expectation-value constraints, $\partial g_a/\partial p_i = g_a(i)$. The
first condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:conditions} then gives
\begin{align*}
    -\ln p_i - 1 - \alpha - \sum_a \lambda_a\, g_a(i) &= 0, \\
    p_i &= e^{-1-\alpha}\, \exp\!\Big(-\sum_a \lambda_a\, g_a(i)\Big).
\end{align*}
Normalization fixes $\alpha$ and yields
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{p_i = \frac{1}{Z}\exp\!\Big(-\sum_a \lambda_a\, g_a(i)\Big),
    \qquad
    Z = \sum_i \exp\!\Big(-\sum_a \lambda_a\, g_a(i)\Big).}
\end{equation}
Since $S$ is strictly concave in $p$ and the constraints are linear, this
stationary point is the unique maximum.
